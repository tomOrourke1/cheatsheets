What changes

  • height check → shape cast
  • set the transform → request a move
  • y < radius → grounded flag
  • Update → PostUpdate

A hand-rolled ground check gets you a ball on a floor. It does not get you walls, ramps, ledges or anything to push. Rapier's kinematic character controller does: you hand it the movement you want, it shape-casts that movement through the world, and returns the movement that actually fits — deflected along surfaces, stepped over small ledges, stopped by anything solid.

You keep authorship of how the character feels. Speed, gravity and jump height stay ordinary numbers in your own code. What you hand over is the geometry problem, which is the part that is genuinely hard to write well.

Note

The NoUserData parameter is for custom physics hooks. You don't need any, so it stays as-is.

Step 04

Build the level

Every solid thing needs two components: a RigidBody that says how it moves, and a Collider that says what shape it is. RigidBody::Fixed means immovable, RigidBody::Dynamic means the simulation owns it.

The level is deliberately awkward: walls to slide along, a ramp to climb, steps to trip over, and crates to shove. Each one exercises a different setting on the character controller in the next step.

Checkpoint

The scene renders with solid meshes, wireframes drawn over them, and the crates settle onto the floor under gravity. Mesh and collider outlines should line up exactly.

Watch out

Collider::cuboid takes half-extents, while Cuboid::new takes full sizes. A wireframe that comes out twice the size of its mesh is always this.

Step 05

The character controller

Now the player. Three components do the work together:

RigidBody::KinematicPositionBased means the simulation never moves this body on its own — you set the position, and it pushes other things rather than being pushed. Collider::ball gives it a shape. KinematicCharacterController is the collide-and-slide solver: you hand it a movement you'd like, and it hands back the movement that actually fits.

Those settings are the whole personality of the character. slide is what turns a wall from a brick into a guide rail. autostep lets the ball walk up the 0.3-unit steps without jumping. snap_to_ground keeps it glued to a downhill slope instead of launching off the crest. max_slope_climb_angle decides which ramps are floors and which are walls.

Note

A ball is an unusual character shape — most games use a capsule, which catches on edges far less. Since the brief is a sphere, autostep is doing the work a capsule's rounded bottom normally would.

Step 08

Ask for movement

Here is the real difference from a hand-rolled controller. You never write to the player's Transform. You set controller.translation to the movement you want, and during PostUpdate Rapier shape-casts that movement through the world, deflects it along whatever it hits, and writes the result to the transform for you.

The vertical_velocity = -1.0 line looks odd but earns its place: a controller that asks for exactly zero vertical movement is not touching the floor, so grounded flickers and snap_to_ground has nothing to grab.

Step 10

Follow the player, in the right order

The camera has to be placed after physics has moved the player, otherwise it chases a position one frame stale and the whole picture jitters. Rapier runs in PostUpdate, so the follow system goes there too — after the writeback that copies physics results into transforms, and before Bevy propagates those transforms for rendering.

Note

This ordering is the fix for the single most common complaint about physics-driven cameras. If a camera ever shudders while the character moves smoothly, it is running in the wrong schedule.

Step 11

Stop the camera clipping through walls

Back the ball into a corner and the camera ends up inside the wall. The fix is a ray cast from the player towards where the camera wants to be: if something is in the way, sit just in front of it instead.

This is also the general shape of every physics query you'll write — a context, a filter, a cast.

Checkpoint

Reverse into a wall. The camera slides in close instead of punching through, and eases back out as you move away.

Note

The same call with cast_shape instead of cast_ray sweeps a sphere rather than a line, which stops the camera clipping corners diagonally. Worth the upgrade once the level has more geometry.

Where to go next

  • Stop climbing on ceilings

    Compare desired_translation.y with effective_translation.y. If you asked to go up and barely moved, you hit something — zero the velocity so the jump ends cleanly.

  • Coyote time

    Keep a timer since grounded was last true and allow a jump for a tenth of a second after. Costs two lines and makes ledges feel fair.

  • Ride moving platforms

    The output's collisions list names every entity you touched. Find the one under your feet and add its movement to yours.

  • Collision groups

    CollisionGroups and SolverGroups decide what collides with what. Use them for trigger volumes, one-way platforms, and ignoring your own projectiles.

  • Fixed timestep

    Rapier defaults to a variable timestep. Switching TimestepMode to fixed makes movement reproducible, which matters as soon as you record replays or go multiplayer.

  • Swap to a capsule

    If the ball catches on geometry more than you'd like, Collider::capsule_y is the shape almost every commercial character uses. Keep the sphere as a child mesh for looks.
