§ 01

The one decision that changes everything

Your Rust file has a comment in it that already knows the answer. Start there.

The C# version stores positions inside triangles and edges. Every comparison is a float comparison, every set lookup is a hash of three floats, and every “is this the same triangle” question is answered approximately. It works in Unity because Vector3.== quietly compares with a tolerance and structs hash by value. It works right up until it doesn't.

Rust refuses to play along. Vec3 has no Eq and no Hash, because f32 has neither, because NaN != NaN. You cannot write HashSet<DelEdge>. That is not Rust being difficult — it is Rust pointing at a bug your C# already has:

So take the note you already left yourself:

the decision
Store points once, in a flat Vec<Vec2>. Everywhere else, refer to them by u32 index. Indices are Copy, Eq, Hash, and 4 bytes. Two vertices are the same vertex when the numbers match — no epsilon, no ambiguity, no abs_diff_eq chains.

The second decision is smaller but worth making now: triangulate in 2D and lift to 3D at the very end. Your Rust already leans this way with plannar / unplannar, but it keeps carrying Vec3 around and flattening repeatedly. Flatten once, on the way in. The whole geometry core then works in Vec2 and you never again wonder which component is being ignored.

§ 04

Points, edges, triangles

Three small types. None of them contain a coordinate.

An edge is an unordered pair. Canonicalising the order at construction is what makes Eq and Hash agree with each other — the thing the C# version got wrong:

A triangle is three indices, always stored counter-clockwise. That invariant is not decoration: the in-circle predicate in § 06 returns a signed value whose sign flips with winding, so if some triangles are clockwise, half your point-in-circle tests come back backwards and you get a mesh full of overlapping garbage.

Note what Tri gets for free. #[derive(PartialEq, Eq)] is now a real, exact equality — no abs_diff_eq, no tolerance. Your Rust impl PartialEq for DelTri compared v1 == other.v1 && ... in order, so a triangle wasn't equal to the same triangle rotated. With indices, if you want rotation-invariant identity you sort the three numbers and compare; usually you don't need to at all.

Projecting Y-up 3D onto (x, z) flips handedness. A loop that is counter-clockwise in your Vec2 space appears clockwise when you look down at the ground from above. This does not affect the triangulation at all — the algorithm only needs to be internally consistent — but it absolutely affects mesh face winding, and it is why the mesh in § 13 emits its indices as (a, c, b). If your generated ground plane is invisible from above and visible from below, this is why.
§ 05

The circumcircle

The circle through three points. Delaunay is defined entirely in terms of it, and you'll want it on screen.

A triangulation is Delaunay when no site lies inside the circumcircle of any triangle. That single property is the whole definition, and it's what makes Delaunay useful: among all possible triangulations of a point set it's the one that maximises the smallest angle, so you get the least-slivery triangles available.

Deriving it

The centre o is equidistant from all three points. Translate so a sits at the origin and let u = b−a, v = c−a. Then |o|² = |o−u|² expands to

2 o · u = |u|²    and    2 o · v = |v|²

which is a 2×2 linear system. Its determinant is uxvy − uyvx — twice the signed area of the triangle — and it is zero exactly when the three points are collinear, which is exactly when there is no circumcircle. Solving gives the code, and because a is on the circle, the radius is just |o|.

shortcut
It is tempting to use Circumcircle::contains as the in-circle test in the main loop. Don't — use § 06 instead. Building the circle costs a division, and near-degenerate triangles push the centre off toward infinity, so you end up comparing two enormous nearly-equal floats and the answer is noise. The determinant in § 06 answers the same question with no division at all. Keep Circumcircle for drawing, for debugging, and for the Voronoi dual (its centres are the Voronoi vertices) — all of which are worth having.
§ 06

Predicates that don't lie

Two determinants. Everything the algorithm decides, it decides with these.

orient2d returns twice the signed area of a triangle: positive for counter-clockwise, negative for clockwise, zero for collinear. in_circle returns a positive value when d falls inside the circle through a counter-clockwise a, b, c. Both are exact expressions — the only error is floating-point rounding.

Widening to f64 is the highest-value line in this file. Your points are f32, so the conversion is exact — you lose nothing — and the subtractions and products that follow, which are where catastrophic cancellation lives, get an enormous amount of extra room. It costs almost nothing and it removes most of the flaky-degenerate-input class of bug outright.

note
It doesn't remove all of it. These are still floating-point predicates, and the true fix is exact arithmetic — Shewchuk's adaptive predicates, available on crates.io as robust. Swapping them in later is a two-function change because you've kept the predicates isolated in their own module. For scattered points in 2D, f64 is almost always enough. For a regular grid in 3D, it is not (§ 16).
§ 07

The super triangle

A triangle big enough to swallow every input point, deleted again at the end.

Bowyer–Watson inserts points one at a time into an existing triangulation, which raises the question of what to insert the first point into. The standard trick is a temporary triangle that contains everything; at the end you throw away any triangle still touching one of its three corners.

Your C# version builds a lopsided triangle from the bounding box, scaled by 10 and 30 in different directions. That works, but it's hard to reason about. An equilateral triangle around the bounding circle is easier to get right: an equilateral triangle with circumradius R has an inradius of R / 2, so a triangle with circumradius 2r is guaranteed to contain any circle of radius r at its centre.

The × 100 is a trade-off with a failure mode on each side, and it's worth knowing both because the symptoms look nothing alike:

Super triangle too smallSuper triangle too large
Triangles near the convex hull keep a super-vertex, get deleted in cleanup, and leave notches missing from the hull. Looks like the algorithm failed. Its vertices are so far away that hull triangles become extremely thin. Their circumcircles are astronomically large and precision degrades. Looks like occasional wrong edges near the boundary.
if you care
A margin of 100× the bounding radius, with f64 predicates underneath, is comfortable for anything game-shaped. If you ever need to be rigorous rather than comfortable, the real answer is ghost vertices: treat the three super-vertices as symbolic points at infinity and special-case the predicates for them, so no numeric margin is involved at all. That's a meaningful rewrite and almost certainly not worth it for procedural level generation.
§ 08

Bowyer–Watson

Insert a point. Delete every triangle that now fails the empty-circle test. Fill the hole. Repeat.

Deleting the bad triangles leaves a star-shaped hole around the new point. The hole's boundary is exactly the set of edges that belonged to one bad triangle — edges shared by two bad triangles are interior to the hole and get deleted with it. Your C# finds those with a nested loop over BadTriangles; a HashMap<Edge, u32> counter does it in one pass, and the canonical Edge from § 04 is what makes the counting correct.

What each step is doing

  1. Partition, don't search. The C# builds a HashSet of bad triangles and then removes them from the list one by one, which is a linear scan per removal on top of broken equality. Draining into two buckets does the same work in one pass with no lookups.
  2. Count, don't compare. Every edge of every bad triangle goes into the map. Count 2 means the edge is interior to the hole. Count 1 means it's on the rim, and it becomes a triangle with the new point.
  3. Trust the invariant. The hole is always star-shaped with respect to the inserted point, so joining the new point to each rim edge always produces a valid triangulation. You never need a point-in-polygon test or a sort around the rim.
note
Points that land exactly on an existing circumcircle make in_circle return zero, and > 0.0 treats them as outside. That's the right call — it keeps the triangulation valid, it just means a cocircular set (four corners of a square, say) resolves its diagonal arbitrarily. Both diagonals are legitimately Delaunay. If your level generator produces grid-aligned rooms you will hit this constantly and it is fine; if you need a specific choice, break the tie on index order rather than nudging the geometry.
§ 09

Cleaning the input

Two duplicate points will produce a zero-area triangle and quietly corrupt everything after it.

Bowyer–Watson has no defence against a repeated site: the second copy sees itself as already present, finds no bad triangles, contributes no cavity edges, and inserts nothing — or worse, produces a degenerate triangle depending on where rounding lands. Scattered random points rarely collide, but points snapped to a grid or read from a tilemap collide constantly.

§ 10

Tests that catch real bugs

The empty-circumcircle property is machine-checkable, and it catches every class of mistake in this algorithm.

Counting triangles proves almost nothing. Checking the defining property proves almost everything: if a winding is backwards, a cavity edge is miscounted, or a predicate sign is flipped, some triangle ends up with a site inside its circumcircle and this test finds it.

shortcut

Wiring it into Bevy

Sites in a resource, the result in another, and change detection deciding when to recompute.

The geometry is a pure function: points in, triangulation out. That maps onto Bevy as two resources and a system that runs only when the input actually changed.

change detection
resource_changed::<Sites> fires on any mutable access, not on an actual value change — taking a ResMut<Sites> and only reading from it will retrigger the whole triangulation every frame. If you have a system that needs mutable access but usually doesn't write, reach for sites.bypass_change_detection(). This is the single most common source of “why is my game doing this work every frame” in Bevy.

§ 15

When it gets slow

The loop above is O(n²). That is completely fine until suddenly it isn't.

Every insertion scans every triangle. At a few hundred sites you won't notice; at ten thousand you will. In rough order of effort-to-payoff:

  1. Check you aren't retriangulating every frame. Ninety percent of the time this is the actual problem, and it's a change-detection bug (§ 11), not an algorithmic one.
  2. Move it off the main thread. AsyncComputeTaskPool plus a component holding the Task, polled each frame. Because triangulate is a pure function over owned data with no ECS references, it moves to another thread with no restructuring — which is the concrete payoff for the module split in § 03.
  3. Spatially sort the input. Insert points in Hilbert-curve or Morton order and consecutive insertions touch nearby triangles, which makes the caching in the next step actually work.
  4. Stop scanning. The real fix is a triangle adjacency structure plus point location by walking from the last inserted triangle toward the new point. That's O(n log n) overall and it's a substantial rewrite of § 08.
shortcut
If you get to step 4 and you're only doing 2D, look at the spade crate first — it's a mature Delaunay implementation with constrained triangulation and Voronoi built in. Writing your own is worth it for understanding and for control over the data layout; it is not worth it if you just need the answer. Nothing about the rest of this tutorial changes: you'd still convert its output into your own Tri indices and everything downstream stays put.
§ 16

The jump to 3D

Hints only, as requested. The algorithm is identical; the arithmetic and the failure modes are not.

Bowyer–Watson generalises to any dimension without changing shape. Everything gets one dimension bigger and the cavity-boundary trick still works — the boundary of the hole is now the set of faces belonging to exactly one bad tetrahedron.

2D3D
Tri { a, b, c }Tet { a, b, c, d }
Edge, sorted pairFace, sorted triple
cavity boundary = edges with count 1cavity boundary = faces with count 1
super trianglesuper tetrahedron
orient2d, 3×3 determinantorient3d, 4×4 determinant
in_circle, 4×4 determinantin_sphere, 5×5 determinant

The two predicates

orient3d(a, b, c, d) is six times the signed volume, and it's just the scalar triple product of the three edge vectors from a. Use it in Tet::new exactly the way orient2d is used in Tri::new — swap two vertices when it's negative, so every tetrahedron has consistent orientation and in_sphere returns a sign you can trust.

in_sphere(a, b, c, d, e) is the same construction one dimension up: subtract e from the other four, and take the 4×4 determinant whose rows are (x, y, z, x²+y²+z²). Positive means e is inside the circumsphere of a positively-oriented (a, b, c, d).

The circumsphere

The derivation is identical to § 05 with a 3×3 system instead of 2×2. With ri = vi − v0:

o = ( |r1|²(r2×r3) + |r2|²(r3×r1) + |r3|²(r1×r2) )  /  2 r1 · (r2×r3)

which is what your existing CircumSphere::new computes — once r2.cross(v3) becomes r2.cross(r3). Centre is v0 + o, radius is |o|, and the denominator is orient3d, so the degenerate case (four coplanar points, no finite sphere) is the same early-out.

Three things that will bite you

  1. Cospherical degeneracy is not an edge case in 3D. The eight corners of a cube lie on one sphere. So do the corners of any axis-aligned box — which is to say, so does every grid-aligned room in a procedural level. in_sphere returns something within rounding of zero and the tetrahedralisation becomes ambiguous. f64 is not enough here; either jitter the input by a tiny random offset, or use exact predicates (robust). Jittering is the pragmatic choice for level generation.
  2. Delaunay does not give you nice tetrahedra. The 2D guarantee — maximise the minimum angle — has no 3D analogue. 3D Delaunay routinely contains slivers: four almost-coplanar points forming a tetrahedron with near-zero volume and a perfectly valid circumsphere. They are mathematically correct and useless for physics or rendering. Filtering them out is a post-process, and it's why mesh generators are a separate field from triangulation.
  3. The count grows. n points in 2D give O(n) triangles. In 3D the worst case is O(n²) tetrahedra, and adversarial-looking inputs (points near a curve) hit it. Budget accordingly before you tetrahedralise ten thousand points.
worth asking
If what you actually want out of 3D is a room graph rather than a volume mesh, consider whether you need tetrahedralisation at all. Extracting edges from a 3D Delaunay and running the same MST from § 14 works and is the standard multi-floor dungeon approach — but so does triangulating each floor in 2D and stitching floors with explicit vertical connections, which is dramatically less code and has no degeneracy problems. Reach for the tetrahedra when you need the volume, not just the connectivity.
