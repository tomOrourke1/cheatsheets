Noise · taxonomy

Pick the right field, then shape it

Almost every "organic" look in procedural generation is one cheap noise function plus three or four cheap transforms. Know what each base field is good at before you start stacking octaves.

Simplex / OpenSimplex2Low–midIsotropic, no lattice bias, scales to 4D cheaply3D/4D volumes, animated fields, anything rotated

The six knobs

KnobWhat it doesTypical
frequencyScale of the input coordinate. noise(p * frequency). Doubling it halves feature size.1/64 … 1/512 in world units
amplitudeOutput scale of one octave.Normalise the sum, not each term

Range discipline

Decide once that every base field returns -1..1 and every fBm returns -1..1, and normalise at the source. Half of all "why is my terrain flat/clipped" bugs are an un-normalised octave sum: the theoretical max of a Perlin sum is Σ amp, so divide by it. Perlin's own bound is sqrt(n)/2 for n dimensions, so multiply raw output by 2/sqrt(n): 1.4142 in 2D, 1.1547 in 3D.

Noise · hashing

Everything starts with a good integer hash

Noise is a hash plus an interpolation scheme. If the hash is bad you get visible repeats, axis correlation, and platform-dependent output. Use an integer bit-mixer, not sin.

Do not use sin-hashes

fract(sin(dot(p, vec2(12.9898, 78.233))) * 43758.5453) is everywhere in shader tutorials and it is a trap: sin precision differs between GPU vendors and drivers, so your "deterministic" world differs per machine, and it degrades badly at large coordinates. Integer mixers are exact everywhere u32 wrapping is defined — which is all of WGSL and all of Rust (wrapping_mul).

Seeding strategy

  • One master seed per world. Derive a distinct u32 per system by hashing the master with a constant: hash_u(master ^ 0xC0FFEE) for caves, ^ 0xBEEF for biomes. Systems then never disturb each other's sequences.
  • Chunked generation must be position-seeded, never sequence-seeded. A chunk's content is a pure function of (world_pos, seed), so it is identical whether you generated it first or thousandth.
  • For sequential randomness inside one chunk, seed a small PRNG (rand_pcg::Pcg32) from hash_i2(chunk.x, chunk.y, seed) rather than the global RNG.
Noise · gradient noise

Perlin, in every dimension you need

Gradient noise: assign a random unit gradient to each lattice corner, take the dot product with the offset from that corner to the sample point, and interpolate with the quintic fade 6t⁵ − 15t⁴ + 10t³ (its first and second derivatives vanish at 0 and 1, so no creases across cell borders).

Going to n dimensions

Perlin generalises directly: 2ⁿ corners, n nested lerps. That exponential is the whole story — 2D is 4 corners, 3D is 8, 4D is 16, 5D is 32. Simplex noise exists precisely to replace that with n+1 corners and O(n²) work.

DimPerlin cornersSimplex cornersWhat you use it for
2D43Heightmaps, masks, flow fields
3D84Caves, volumetrics, animated 2D (z = time)
4D165Seamlessly tiling 2D (torus), animated 3D
5D+32+6+Tiling 3D volumes; rare, and simplex-only in practice
The cheap trick

You rarely need real nD noise. To animate a 2D field, sample 3D noise at (x, y, t). To seamlessly tile a 2D field, sample 4D noise on a torus (see Seamless & spherical). To texture a sphere, sample 3D noise at the surface position. Dimensions are a coordinate trick far more often than a noise-function requirement.

Analytic derivatives

If you need normals, slope masks, or erosion-style fBm, do not finite-difference — carry the derivative through. For 2D Perlin the derivative of the interpolation is available in closed form; the practical shortcut is the derivative fade 30t⁴ − 60t³ + 30t². Returning vec3(value, ddx, ddy) from your noise function costs perhaps 20% more and buys exact normals plus the uber-noise family in the cookbook.

Noise · simplex

Simplex, OpenSimplex2 and when to bother

Perlin's lattice is a square grid, so features align to axes and diagonals — obvious once you fBm it hard or warp it. Simplex noise uses a skewed triangular (simplex) lattice: fewer corners, isotropic gradients, and no directional bias. In 2D the visual difference is modest; in 3D/4D it is both faster and cleaner.

  • Ken Perlin's simplex noise patent expired in 2022 — the old licensing worry is gone. That said, OpenSimplex2 (the OpenSimplex2S/2F variants) has better visual quality than classic simplex in 3D and is what most modern libraries ship.
  • Writing simplex from scratch in WGSL is a page of skew constants and corner ranking. Unless you enjoy that, port a known-good implementation or use a library on the CPU side.
  • Cheap middle ground: keep Perlin, but rotate each octave by an irrational angle and offset it. Rotating octaves destroys the lattice alignment that makes Perlin fBm look like Perlin fBm — see fbm_rot in the cookbook.

Parity matters

If a crate generates your collision heightmap on the CPU while a WGSL function shades the terrain on the GPU, they must agree exactly or you get visible skating between the visual and physical surface. Either (a) port one hash to both sides, as in this guide, or (b) generate on one side only and upload a texture/buffer to the other. Do not eyeball two implementations into agreement.

Noise · cellular

Voronoi & Worley: F1, F2, IDs and borders

Jitter one feature point into every lattice cell, then for a sample point search the 3×3 neighbourhood. F1 is the distance to the nearest point, F2 the second nearest. Everything cellular comes out of combining those two plus the cell's identity.

True border distance

f2 − f1 approximates edges but its width varies with cell size. For a geometrically correct distance to the Voronoi border — crisp, uniform cracks — run a second pass measuring the distance to the perpendicular bisector between the nearest point and each neighbour.

VariantHowLook
Metric swapReplace dot(r,r) with abs(r.x)+abs(r.y) (Manhattan) or max(abs(r.x),abs(r.y)) (Chebyshev)Diamond / square cells — good for crystals, city blocks
Smooth VoronoiAccumulate exp(-k*d) over the neighbourhood, then -log(sum)/kNo hard creases; differentiable, so it plays well with lighting
VoronoiseBlend the weighting between "nearest wins" and "average of all" with one parameterA continuous dial from Voronoi to value noise
jitter < 1Scale the offset toward the cell centreAt 0 it is a perfect square grid — useful for stylised tiling
3D Worley27-cell search with vec3Rock volumes, cave carving, cloud detail
Search radius

A 3×3 search is only correct while jitter keeps every feature point inside its own cell. If you push points outside their cells, or you need exact F2 in pathological configurations, widen to 5×5 — at nearly 3× the cost. For dense scattering (trees, rocks) prefer Poisson-disc sampling, which gives you the same evenness without the per-pixel search.

When the field is too big: jump flooding

For a full-screen or full-map Voronoi/distance field over thousands of seeds, do not evaluate per pixel. Seed a texture with your points and run the Jump Flood Algorithm: log₂(n) compute passes with halving step sizes, each pixel taking the best of 9 samples at offset k. Result: an approximate nearest-seed map plus a signed distance field in ~10 passes at 1024². This is the standard tool for region maps, outlines and SDF generation on the GPU.

Shaping · cookbook

Modifier cookbook

The base field is raw material. These are the transforms that turn one Perlin call into mountains, canyons, marble, cracked mud or cave systems. They compose — most good-looking terrain is three of them stacked.

Fractal sums

Derivative-aware fBm (the erosion look)

Real mountains have smooth valley floors and sharp ridges because deposition fills flat areas. You can fake it for free: carry the analytic gradient and damp each octave by how steep the terrain already is. This is dramatically cheaper than a hydraulic erosion simulation and gets you 70% of the way.

Remaps: cheap, high impact

Combination patterns

RecipeConstruction
Mountains + plainsBlend ridged and fbm with a third, very low-frequency noise as the mask. Use smoothstep on the mask so the transition is a foothill band, not a line.
Modulated amplitudeheight = fbm(p) * (0.2 + 0.8 * fbm(p * 0.1, other_seed)). Instant regional variety in relief.
Caves3D ridged noise thresholded: solid = perlin3(p) > -0.2 + ridged3(p) * 0.6. Tune with depth so caves close near the surface.
Perlin wormsTake 1 − abs(noise3) and keep only values above ~0.93. The thin shells that survive are winding tunnels. Two independent worms intersecting gives junctions.
Riversabs(fbm) near zero is a network of thin channels. Carve with smoothstep and use the same field to place water.
Cracked mudvoronoi_border thresholded, warped by a small fBm, multiplied by a large-scale mask so cracks only appear in dry regions.
Biome regionsVoronoi cell ID for the region, f2 − f1 for the blend width at the boundary. Warp the input first so borders are not polygonal.
Scattering weightsLow-frequency fBm as a density field, sampled by a Poisson/blue-noise point set. Density controls acceptance, blue noise controls spacing.
Shaping · warping

Domain warping: the single highest-value trick

Instead of changing the noise, change where you sample it. Feed a noise-derived offset into the coordinate, and straight fractal mush becomes swirling, geological, hand-drawn-looking structure. One extra fBm evaluation per axis; enormous payoff.

Tuning

Warp amount is in the same units as the coordinate you feed the noise. Start at 0.3–0.6 of one feature wavelength; beyond ~1.0 the field folds over itself and the fractal structure dissolves into paisley. Use a lower-octave fBm for the warp than for the final field — the warp only needs shape, not detail, and this halves the cost.

Shaping · topology

Seamless tiles, spheres and animation

Three coordinate tricks solve nearly every "my noise has a seam" problem. None of them require a different noise function — only a different input space.

ProblemFix
Seam between chunksSample in world space, never chunk-local space. A chunk at (10, 4) samples noise(chunk_origin + local). Nine times out of ten this is the entire bug.
Precision wobble far from originAt 10⁶ world units f32 has ~0.06 unit precision. Subtract a large integer offset before sampling, or keep the lattice cell as i32 and only the fraction as f32 — which the integer-hash formulation above already does.
Visible repeats in a tiling textureTile at a large period and break the repetition with a second, non-tiling low-frequency variation, or use stochastic texture sampling.
Pinching at sphere polesNever UV-map noise onto a sphere. Sample 3D noise at the direction vector.
Engine · wiring

Getting it into Bevy

Two routes, and you will usually want both: build a Mesh on the CPU when the geometry itself must exist (collision, gameplay queries, navmesh), and evaluate noise in WGSL when it is only ever pixels.

CPU: heightmap to Mesh

RenderAssetUsages

RenderAssetUsages::default() keeps the mesh in both the main world and the render world. If you never read the vertices again (no collider, no raycast, no CPU picking), pass RenderAssetUsages::RENDER_WORLD alone and Bevy drops the CPU copy after upload — a real memory saving across hundreds of terrain chunks. If you do build colliders from the mesh, keep MAIN_WORLD, or better: keep the raw height array and build the collider from that instead of from the mesh.

GPU: a noise material

Shader libraries

Do not paste your noise into six shaders. Bevy's shader pipeline supports imports, so put the hash and noise functions in one file and reuse them:

Which side should generate?

NeedWhere
Terrain you can walk onCPU. Physics needs vertex data; keep the height array, not just the mesh.
Millions of pixels of surface detailGPU fragment shader. Never bake what you can evaluate.
Huge voxel/marching-cubes volumesGPU compute writing to a storage buffer, read back only if gameplay needs it. Read-back is the expensive part — design to avoid it.
Gameplay decisions (biome at a point, spawn tables)CPU, and it must match what the GPU draws — same hash, same constants.
One-off texturesGenerate once into an Image asset at startup, or bake offline. Do not re-evaluate 8 octaves per pixel per frame for a static texture.
Engine · streaming

Chunks without frame hitches

Generating a chunk on the main schedule stalls the frame. Push it to the task pool, poll for completion, and spawn when the mesh is ready.

TrapFix
Cracks between LOD levelsAdd a skirt: extend the chunk border downward by more than the maximum height delta. Ugly, invisible, and free. Stitching index buffers is the correct fix but rarely worth it.
Seams in normals at bordersAnalytic normals from the noise gradient are continuous across chunks by construction. Finite-difference normals are not, unless you sample one vertex beyond the border.
Frame spike on spawnCap how many chunks you accept per frame in finish_chunks — .take(2) on the iterator is usually enough.
Chunks generated in a different order give different worldsYou seeded a sequential RNG. Position-seed everything.
Grids · the main event

The Townscaper grid, step by step

Oskar Stålberg's grid looks impossible — an infinite, deterministic, irregular field of near-square quads with no repeating pattern. The construction is four operations long, and none of them is hard.

Stålberg described the technique publicly, and Boris the Brave's Sylves tutorial works through a full implementation. Reduced to its four operations: build a hexagon of equilateral triangles, dissolve random pairs of them into quads, subdivide every remaining face into quads, and relax. The infinite version tiles hexagonal chunks and relaxes across the assembled result rather than per chunk.

  1. Start with a triangle lattice, bounded to a hexagon

    Two basis vectors 60° apart. Each lattice rhombus splits into an "up" and a "down" triangle. Bound it with the axial hex test |q| ≤ R ∧ |r| ≤ R ∧ |q+r| ≤ R, which carves a regular hexagon out of the lattice — the shape matters because hexagons tile the plane and have no long straight edges to leave visible scars.

    Why triangles? Because a triangulation is the one thing you can always merge into quads. Start from squares and you can only ever get squares.

  2. Randomly merge pairs of triangles into quads

    This is a random maximal matching on the dual graph: each interior edge is a candidate to be dissolved, and each triangle can only be consumed once. Shuffle the edges, walk the list, dissolve an edge whenever both of its triangles are still free.

    You will not match everything — roughly one triangle in ten is left unmatched, and that is fine, because the next step turns any polygon into quads. Chasing a perfect matching (Blossom algorithm) is not worth it.

    The trick that buys you infinity: instead of shuffling, give every edge a priority from hash(endpoint lattice coords, seed) and process edges in priority order. The outcome for any given edge then depends only on its immediate neighbourhood, not on a global shuffle order — so two chunks generated independently, in any order, on any machine, agree along their shared border.

  3. Subdivide everything into quads

    The Conway ortho operator, which is one step of Catmull–Clark topology: add a centroid to each face and a midpoint to each edge, then emit one quad per corner — [corner, next edge midpoint, centroid, previous edge midpoint]. An n-gon becomes n quads, so quads become 4 and leftover triangles become 3. After this step the mesh is 100% quads and every vertex has valence 3, 4 or 6.

    Edge midpoints must be shared between the two faces that touch the edge, or the mesh comes apart. Key them by the sorted vertex pair.

  4. Relax until the quads look square

    Plain Laplacian smoothing (move each vertex toward the average of its neighbours) shrinks the mesh and gives you rhombi. The version that produces the Townscaper look fits a square to each quad and pulls the corners toward it:

    For a quad with centroid c, corner i should sit 90°·i around c from corner 0. So rotate each pᵢ − c back by −90°·i, average the four results into one vector, then rotate it forward again to get four target corners. That average is the best-fitting square, at the right size and orientation, for free — no trigonometry, just index swizzles and sign flips. Each vertex then moves toward the average of the targets contributed by every quad it belongs to.

    10–20 iterations at a step of ~0.5 is plenty. Pin the outer boundary if the grid is finite, or let it breathe if it is a chunk interior.

The code

Making it infinite

The single-hex version above is enough for most games — generate one big hex (or a rectangle of them) at startup and never think about it again. If you genuinely need an unbounded world, there are two workable routes.

RouteHow it works
Chunked + global relaxFill a hex grid of chunks. Each chunk generates its own triangles and pairing with a seed derived from its hex coordinate, so it is deterministic; the meshes are welded together by position; and the relaxation runs across the whole assembled region, not per chunk. Relaxing chunks independently is what produces visible chunk seams. This is exactly how the Sylves library builds its Townscaper grid, and its relax modifier operates on the infinite grid rather than the individual meshes.
Local determinism throughoutHash-priority matching (step 2 above) removes the need to seed per chunk at all. Combine it with a windowed relaxation: to produce chunk C, build C plus two rings of neighbours, relax the whole window, keep only C. Relaxation is a local, contracting process — beyond two rings the influence is below float noise — so neighbouring windows agree to within a pixel and the seams vanish.
Welding

Whatever you do, weld duplicate vertices before relaxing. Quantise the position to a fixed grid ((p / eps).round() as i64, with eps ≈ 1/1000 of the triangle side) and use that as a hash key. An unwelded mesh will look fine as triangles and then tear itself apart the moment you relax it.

Refinements worth adding

  • Vary the generator per chunk. The real Townscaper grid occasionally swaps in a different mesh generator for a hex, which breaks up the statistical sameness of pure random matching.
  • Bias the matching. Prefer merges that produce quads closer to square (compare the two diagonals) by folding a shape score into the priority. You get fewer bad cells before relaxation even starts.
  • Punish valence-6 vertices. Vertices where six quads meet are the ugliest artefact. Detect them and either re-roll the local matching or weight them harder during relaxation.
  • Go spherical. The algorithm is topological — nothing in it assumes a plane. Start from a subdivided icosahedron instead of a hex of triangles, run the identical four steps, and project vertices back onto the sphere after each relaxation iteration. You get a Townscaper grid on a planet.
  • Store the dual. Keep the corner graph (which quads touch each vertex) as well as the face graph. Tile selection needs corners; pathfinding and adjacency need faces.

Grids · payoff

Putting tiles on irregular quads

An irregular quad grid is only interesting because you can still drop authored geometry onto it. The grid is topologically a square grid — every cell has four corners and four neighbours — so all the square-grid machinery still applies. Only the positions are irregular.

Corner-driven tiles (the marching-squares family)

Store your game state on the corners, not the faces. A cell's appearance is then determined by its four corner values. With booleans that is 2⁴ = 16 configurations, which collapse under the square's symmetry group (4 rotations × 2 reflections) to 6 canonical cases: empty, one corner, two adjacent, two diagonal, three, four. Author six meshes, place them rotated or mirrored to match.

  • The diagonal case is genuinely ambiguous — two corners set, two clear, and the surface can connect either way. Pick a convention and apply it everywhere, or you get flickering geometry when a neighbour changes.
  • Stack floors and it becomes a marching-cubes problem: 2⁸ = 256 corner configurations per cube. Townscaper's geometry is effectively 2.5D — a 2D corner grid extruded into columns — so per-floor 2D logic plus vertical connectors is usually enough, and far fewer meshes to author.
  • Want less authoring still? Use a dual grid: offset the tile grid by half a cell so each tile covers one corner of four neighbours. Fewer tiles cover more configurations.

Deforming a tile into a cell

Cells are not squares, so the tile mesh must bend. Bilinear interpolation of the four corners does it, and because adjacent cells share corner positions, adjacent tiles line up exactly with no cracks.

Two refinements

For stacked floors, interpolate trilinearly between the cell's footprint and its (possibly differently relaxed) top face. And bend the tile's normals with the inverse-transpose of the local Jacobian if you want lighting to be exactly right — in practice, recomputing smooth normals after deformation is close enough and much simpler.

Wave function collapse on an irregular grid

WFC does not care about geometry, only adjacency. Give it the face graph of your quad grid — each cell, its four neighbours, and which edge of the cell each neighbour sits across — and the standard algorithm runs unchanged:

  1. Every cell starts with the full set of tile options.
  2. Collapse the lowest-entropy cell (fewest remaining options) to one tile, weighted by authored frequency.
  3. Propagate: remove from each neighbour any option whose socket does not match, and keep propagating outward while anything changes.
  4. On contradiction, backtrack, or restart the local region. Chunk the propagation so a failure never restarts the whole map.

The practical work in WFC is not the solver — it is the socket taxonomy. Give each tile edge a symbol, mark which symbols are symmetric and which come in mirrored pairs, and generate the rotations programmatically instead of authoring 4 variants of everything by hand.

Wider net · toolbox

The rest of the toolbox

Noise and grids cover a lot, but not everything. This is the shortlist of techniques worth knowing they exist, and what each one is actually for.

Structure & layout

TechniqueUse it for
Wave function collapseLocal-constraint tiling: buildings, dungeons, decorated grids. Excellent texture; no global structure — it cannot guarantee "the exit is reachable".
Graph grammarsLayouts that must satisfy gameplay rules (lock before key). Generate a graph of rooms and dependencies first, embed it in space second.
BSP / rooms-and-corridorsFast, readable dungeon floors. Boring alone, great as a skeleton to decorate with WFC or CA.
Cellular automataCave systems. Random fill at ~45%, then 4–5 smoothing passes of "become the majority of your neighbours". Flood-fill afterwards to discard disconnected pockets.
Poisson-disc (Bridson)Even scatter with a guaranteed minimum distance, in O(n). The correct answer for trees, rocks and props — far better than rejection sampling.
Lloyd relaxationTurning random points into evenly spaced ones. Iterate: build Voronoi, move each point to its cell's centroid. Two or three passes is plenty.
Delaunay + Voronoi polygon mapsContinent/region maps with real coastlines, rivers and biomes computed per-polygon rather than per-pixel.
L-systems / space colonisationTrees, roots, river networks, lightning. Space colonisation gives more natural branching than pure rewriting.
Wang / corner tilesNon-repeating texture and terrain tiling from a small authored set. Corner-based ("blob") tilesets need 47 tiles for full 8-neighbour coverage; edge-based need 16.

Surfaces & volumes

TechniqueUse it for
Marching squares / cubesExtracting a surface from a scalar field. Simple, well-documented, produces poor topology at sharp features.
Surface nets / dual contouringBetter than marching cubes for editable voxel terrain: one vertex per cell, and dual contouring preserves sharp edges using the field's gradient (Hermite data).
Greedy meshingBlocky voxel worlds. Merges coplanar faces into large quads; typically 5–10× fewer triangles.
Hydraulic erosionThe thing that makes terrain look real. Droplet simulation: spawn particles, carry sediment downhill, deposit in flats. Thousands of droplets over a heightmap, easily on the GPU. Thermal erosion (slope-limited material slide) is cheaper and fixes impossibly steep cliffs.
Triplanar mappingTexturing procedural geometry that has no sensible UVs. Blend three world-space projections by the squared normal.
Jump floodDistance fields, Voronoi regions, outlines, and "distance to nearest X" queries — all in a handful of compute passes.

Control

  • Separate the generator from the arbiter. Generate cheaply, then score the result against constraints and re-roll. Rejection sampling with a good scoring function beats a clever generator you cannot reason about.
  • Author the exceptions. Hand-place landmarks, then let the procedural layer fill and blend around them. Every good procedural game does this.
  • Make it seeded and shareable. A world you can reproduce from a short string is a debugging tool, a bug report format, and a social feature at once.
  • Build the visualiser first. A window that dumps the intermediate field as a texture pays for itself within an hour. Almost every procedural bug is invisible in a debugger and obvious in a picture.

Gotchas checklist

SymptomCause
Terrain looks washed out / flatUn-normalised octave sum, or you clamped before scaling. Divide by Σ amp.
Visible grid alignment in fBmPerlin's lattice showing through. Rotate and offset each octave, or use simplex.
Same pattern repeats every N unitsHash collapsing — usually a weak hash or seed added to the coordinate instead of mixed into the hash.
Seams between chunksSampling in chunk-local coordinates. Sample in world space.
Noise degrades far from originf32 precision. Keep the integer lattice cell in i32 and only the fractional part in f32.
GPU and CPU disagreeDifferent hash, or a sin-based hash. Port one integer mixer to both.
Everything is mirrored / inside outWinding order. Bevy wants counter-clockwise front faces; check with cross(b−a, c−a) pointing where you expect.
floor vs casti32(x) truncates toward zero, so -0.5 becomes 0, not -1. Always floor first, then cast. Same for the fractional part: use p - floor(p), never p % 1.0.
Modulo of negatives(-3) % 8 is -3 in both Rust and WGSL. For wrapping use ((a % n) + n) % n.
Relaxation explodes or collapsesUnwelded duplicate vertices, or a step size above ~0.9. Weld first, keep the step at 0.5.
Mesh disappears at certain anglesNo bounding box / AABB computed from stale vertex data after you mutated positions.
Generation stutters every few secondsAllocating per chunk. Reuse buffers, and cap chunks accepted per frame.
